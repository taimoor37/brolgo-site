Rebuild or refactor? What to do when your MVP starts to struggle

By Taimoor Ahmed · 6 min read

Your MVP did its job. People signed up, some of them paid, and now you're getting feature requests faster than you can ship them. Then things start to creak. Pages get slow. Every new feature breaks something old. Your developer sighs every time you ask for a change.

At this point most founders ask the same question: do we fix what we have, or start again? Here's how we help clients answer it.

Signs your MVP is hitting its limits

  • Small changes take far too long. A simple tweak turns into a week because everything is tangled together.
  • Fixing one bug creates two more. There are no automated tests, so nobody knows what a change will break.
  • It slows down as you grow. Pages that loaded instantly with 50 users now crawl with 2,000.
  • You can't add what customers are asking for. Team accounts, roles, integrations or multiple customers on one platform just don't fit the way it was built.
  • Only one person understands it, or the AI tool that built it, and nobody wants to touch it.

Seeing two or three of these is normal. It means the MVP worked. It doesn't automatically mean you need to throw it away.

When refactoring is the right call

Refactoring means improving the code you have, piece by piece, while the product keeps running. It's usually the better choice when:

  • The tech stack is sensible and well supported, even if the code is messy.
  • The problems sit in a few specific areas, like one slow page or one fragile feature.
  • The database structure mostly makes sense for where the product is heading.
  • You need to keep shipping features while things get better.

Most AI built apps from tools like Lovable or Bolt fall into this group. The foundations are often fine. What's missing is structure, tests, security and proper hosting. If that sounds like yours, here's how to move off Lovable without starting again.

When a rebuild makes more sense

A rebuild means writing a new version alongside the old one, then moving customers across. It's worth considering when:

  • The product has fundamentally changed since the MVP, for example from a single business tool to a multi customer SaaS platform.
  • It's built on a no code or low code platform you've outgrown, with limits you can't work around.
  • The database design blocks the features your customers need most.
  • Security or compliance problems run through the whole app, not just a few places.

Rebuilds take longer and cost more, so be honest about whether the problem is the code or simply the backlog.

The middle path most teams should take

In practice, the best answer is usually a bit of both. Keep the product running, then replace the weakest parts one at a time. Start with whatever hurts most, often the database, logins and permissions, or the slowest screens. Add tests around each part as you go, so it stays fixed.

This way you never have a six month freeze where customers see nothing new, and every step makes the product easier to grow.

Before you decide, get a proper look

The worst decisions get made on gut feel. A short review of the code, database, hosting and the features on your roadmap will tell you clearly where the real problems are and what each option would cost.

Common questions

How do I know if my MVP needs a rebuild?

A rebuild is worth considering when the product has fundamentally changed, you've hit hard limits of a no code platform, or the database design blocks the features customers need most. Otherwise, refactoring piece by piece is usually cheaper and safer.

How much does it cost to rebuild an MVP?

A rebuild typically costs more and takes longer than targeted refactoring, often similar to a medium app build. See our app cost guide for 2026 ranges in Australia.

Can you scale an MVP without stopping new features?

Yes. Replace the weakest parts one at a time while the product keeps running, starting with whatever hurts most, and add tests as you go so each fix stays fixed.
